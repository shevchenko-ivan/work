Diez — a chord service for guitar players, built end to end.

My own product: a free, ad-free service for finding song chords, live at diez.net.ua, with an iOS app in beta. I own the whole loop — positioning, design, code, analytics — and pay for every decision myself.

Why this product exists.

I play guitar. The sites where I looked up chords were cluttered with ads or not made for a Ukrainian-speaking audience. There was room for a quiet, focused service built around one flow: find the song, open it, play.

There is a second reason. A designer who advises product teams should own a full product loop at least once — from positioning to the hosting bill. Diez is how I practise what I recommend.

Product decisions.

  • One core flowFind, open, play. Every feature is judged by whether it shortens that path.
  • Mobile firstA guitar player reaches for a phone, not a laptop. The interface is designed for one hand and a glance.
  • A catalogue that helps you chooseSearch by song, artist or lyrics, artist pages, saved songs and themed collections such as songs for beginners.
  • Privacy by defaultAnalytics starts only after a visitor accepts cookies, and the iOS app ships with a privacy manifest. I accept seeing fewer users in the data as the price of asking first.
  • Free and without adsThe service is free. Monetisation is modelled — using the unit-economics framework I studied at Projector — but deliberately postponed until retention is healthy.

What the numbers taught me.

In the first month after the public launch the service brought in about 1,000 new users. Most of them came directly, and only a fifth to a quarter from organic search.

  • Priorities changedI moved effort from new features to search visibility and content, because that is where growth can sustain itself.
  • Retention before monetisationProduct analytics showed that returning users are the weak spot. That, not a paywall, is the next problem to solve.
  • Design and code in one loopA decision made in Figma is tested in the browser the same day. It makes me a more realistic partner for engineering teams.

It is a small product, and I say so. What it gives a team that works with me is first-hand experience of product economics, analytics and the real cost of every “small” design decision.

What building Diez keeps teaching me.

  • Scope is designEvery screen costs engineering time. Cutting is a design decision, not laziness.
  • Traffic sources are a design inputHow people arrive changes what the first screen must do.
  • Measure before you monetiseA paywall on a product people do not return to solves nothing.
