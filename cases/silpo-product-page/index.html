Silpo product page — two ways to hold the same facts.

A research-led iteration on the product page of Silpo’s online shop. Two Figma prototypes — information grouped in accordions vs in chips — tested in moderated sessions with 18 guests on their phones.

Year
2023
Company
Fozzy Group · Silpo
Role
Product designer · co-designed the prototypes
Scope
Product page · mobile web

The page guests open before they commit.

A grocery product page carries a lot: price, discount, nutrition, composition, description, storage, promo terms. Our first screen went picture → title → price → discount → nutrition → description → composition, with everything else tucked into groups.

Nobody had checked whether that order — or grouping at all — matched what guests actually look for. So instead of arguing about layouts, we wrote the assumptions down and tested them.

Three assumptions worth breaking.

  1. Maybe grouping isn’t neededAccordions and chips add taps. Would one long page serve guests better?
  2. Maybe the order is wrongWhat does a guest expect to find on the page — and what do they look for first?
  3. Maybe promo terms don’t matterPerhaps guests only want to see the benefit, not the conditions.

Two prototypes, 18 guests, one phone each.

  • Prototype A vs BThe same content, grouped in accordions (A) or in chips (B). Built in Figma and clicked through on a phone.
  • Two segments of nineRegular online shoppers who are not price-sensitive, and active promo hunters buying fresh categories. Ages 25–45, big cities, ordering through mobile web at least twice a month.
  • Moderated usability sessionsRun together with Silpo’s guest-experience research team. Success measured by comments, the number of hints a guest needed, and a 1–5 rating.

The container mattered less than the content.

18Guests tested
8/9Liked the accordion grouping
1/18Wanted one long block

Every guest found what they needed in both prototypes. Accordions or chips — guests didn’t care. They cared that the facts were there and easy to find.

  • Grouping staysOnly one guest out of 18 wanted everything in a single block. The preferred order: description, composition, nutrition, characteristics.
  • Facts are the must-havesComposition with allergens, country of origin, producer, energy and nutrition — including sugar and salt — and markers like kosher or vegan.
  • Marketing copy is noise for staplesGuests know what milk is. A description earns its place only for new or specific products: wine, cakes, delicacies.
  • Guests read the discount, not the termsThe size of the discount and the benefit came first. General promo copy on the page read as advertising, not as information about this product.

What the test handed to the team.

  • Add what’s missingAllergens, added sugar, vegan and lactose-free markers, price per kg or litre, the weight of one piece for goods sold by weight.
  • Remove what misleadsShelf life without a production date means nothing to a guest. The marketing description goes away for staples.
  • Upgrade the small cardShow the full product name without truncation and add stock availability to the catalogue card.

What I took from it.

  • Test the assumption, not the layoutAccordions vs chips was the question the team argued about most — and the one that mattered least.
  • Content design beats container designThe biggest wins were facts we added or removed, not components we swapped.
